# Amendment of the Constitution and the Basic Structure Doctrine (Article 368)

Article 368 in Part XX gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution. There are three methods of amendment. Some provisions can be amended by a simple majority of Parliament, such as the creation of new States under Article 3. Most provisions require a special majority, meaning a majority of the total membership of each House and at least two-thirds of the members present and voting. Provisions that affect the federal structure, such as the election of the President, the powers of the Supreme Court and the distribution of legislative powers, need a special majority and also ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the States. After the Bill is passed, the President must give assent. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a bench of thirteen judges held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its basic structure. The doctrine of the basic structure was applied again in Minerva Mills (1980), and it remains the principal limit on the amending power of Parliament.
