# EPA Drinking Water Treatment Process

Conventional drinking water treatment in a municipal plant removes particles and microorganisms in several stages. In coagulation, chemicals such as alum are added to raw water so that small particles clump together. In flocculation the water is gently mixed so that these clumps grow into larger particles called floc. In sedimentation the heavy floc settles to the bottom of a basin. The clearer water then passes through filters made of sand, gravel and charcoal, which remove smaller particles, dust, parasites and bacteria. In the last step, disinfection, a disinfectant such as chlorine, chloramine, ultraviolet light or ozone kills remaining germs, and a small residual disinfectant is kept in the pipes to protect water on its way to customers. The Safe Drinking Water Act of 1974 authorises the Environmental Protection Agency to set national standards for contaminants known as maximum contaminant levels. Utilities must test water regularly and report results to customers in an annual consumer confidence report. Turbidity, which measures the cloudiness of water, is monitored because it can shelter microorganisms from disinfection.
